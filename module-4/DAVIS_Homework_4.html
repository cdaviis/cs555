The data is from a Canadian 1970 census which collected information about specific occupations. Data collected was used to develop a regression model to predict prestige for all occupations. Use R or Python to calculate the quantities and generate the visual summaries requested below.

(1) To get a sense of the data, generate a scatterplot to examine the association between prestige score and years of education. Briefly describe the form, direction, and strength of the association between the variables. Calculate the correlation coefficient. (3 points)

Education is on the x-axis and prestige is on the y-axis.

The points show a positive association. Occupations with more education tend to have higher prestige. The form looks roughly linear, and the relationship is strong. The correlation is r = 0.850. See appendix, question 1.

(2) Perform a simple linear regression with prestige score and years of education, and briefly summarize your conclusions (no need to do the 5-step procedure here). Generate a residual plot. Assess whether the model assumptions are met. Are there any outliers or influence points? If so, identify them by ID and comment on the effect of each on the regression. (4 points)

Predicted prestige = -10.732 + 5.361 × (years of education).

Each extra year of education is associated with about 5.36 more prestige points. R-squared = 0.723, so education explains about 72.3% of the variation in prestige. The education slope is statistically significant (t = 16.15, p < 0.0001).

A straight line is a decent fit, but the residuals bend a little (more positive at the low and high ends, more negative in the middle), so linearity isn't perfect. The spread looks about the same across the fitted values, so constant variance seems fine. The Q-Q plot follows the line except in the tails, so normality is roughly okay. I can't check independence from a plot; it depends on how the data were collected.

Outliers (|studentized residual| > 2): IDs 41, 46, 53, 54, and 67. FILE_CLERKS (41), COLLECTORS (46), and SERVICE_STATION_ATTENDANT (54) have unusually negative residuals, but their Cook's distances do not exceed the 4/n screening threshold.

Influential points (Cook's D > 4/n): IDs 24, 53, and 67.

  • ID 24 PHYSICIANS: omitting it lowers the slope from 5.361 to 5.271.
  • ID 53 NEWSBOYS: omitting it lowers the slope to 5.322.
  • ID 67 FARMERS: omitting it raises the slope to 5.458.

The slope stays positive and changes little in every case, so I keep all observations. See appendix, question 2.

(3) Calculate the least squares regression equation that predicts prestige score from education, income, and percentage of women. Formally test (using the 5-step procedure) whether the set of these predictors are associated with prestige score at the α = 0.05 level (Hint: You should be performing the global test). (3 points)

Predicted prestige = -6.794 + 4.187 × Education + 0.001314 × Income - 0.00891 × Women.

  1. Null hypothesis: all three slopes are 0. Alternative: at least one slope is not 0. Alpha = 0.05.
  2. Global F statistic. Numerator df = 3, denominator df = 98.
  3. Reject H0 if F > 2.697; otherwise, fail to reject H0.
  4. F = 129.19. The p-value is about 6.26 × 10^-34.
  5. F is greater than 2.697, so reject H0. The set of predictors is associated with prestige.

R-squared = 0.798. Adjusted R-squared = 0.792. See appendix, question 3.

(4) If the overall model was significant, summarize the information about the contribution of each variable separately at the same significance level as used for the overall model (no need to do a formal 5-step procedure for each one, just comment on the results of the tests). Provide interpretations for any estimates (of the slopes) that are significant. Calculate 95% confidence intervals for any estimates that are significant. (3 points)

Education: t = 10.771, p < 0.0001. Significant. Holding income and percentage of women fixed, one more year of education is associated with about 4.187 more prestige points. 95% CI: (3.415, 4.958).

Income: t = 4.729, p < 0.0001. Significant. Holding education and percentage of women fixed, an additional 1,000 dollars of income is associated with about 1.314 more prestige points. The 95% confidence interval is (0.762, 1.865) prestige points per 1,000 dollars.

Women: t = -0.293, p = 0.770. Not significant. After accounting for education and income, there is not enough evidence that percentage of women is associated with prestige. See appendix, question 4.

(5) Generate a residual plot showing the fitted values from the regression against the residuals. Is the fit of the model reasonable? Are there any outliers or influence points? (3 points)

The fit looks reasonable. Residuals are scattered around 0 with no clear curve or funnel shape, and R-squared is about 0.80. The residuals look roughly symmetric, though a few large ones (FARMERS, NEWSBOYS) sit in the tails.

Outliers (|studentized residual| > 2): IDs 29, 46, 53, 67, and 82.

High leverage (h > 2p/n): IDs 2, 17, 20, 24, and 84.

Influential points (Cook's D > 4/n): IDs 2, 20, 24, 27, 29, 41, 53, 54, 67, and 82. ID 2 GENERAL_MANAGERS has the largest Cook's distance (about 0.283). Omitting it changes the education slope from 4.187 to 3.947 and the income slope from 0.001314 to 0.001601. I keep all observations, but the coefficients are sensitive to ID 2. See appendix, question 5.

(6) Examine if there is a linear interaction between income and education level (examine if the interaction is significant)? (2 points)

I add an Education × Income term to the model.

The interaction estimate is -0.000203. t = -2.623, p = 0.0101. At alpha = 0.05, the interaction is significant.

So the association between income and prestige depends on education. R-squared goes from 0.798 to 0.812. See appendix, question 6.

(7) Fill in the gaps in the following multiple answer question:

As more independent variables are added to a multiple regression model, ___________ will increase; this is not always so with ___________, which will only increase if the additional variables add substantial explanatory power to the model.

A) R²; adjusted R²

B) adjusted R²; R²

C) R²; the coefficient of determination

D) the standard error of the estimate; R²

Explain why? (2 points)

Answer: A) R²; adjusted R²

R-squared cannot go down when you add predictors, because the larger model can always match the smaller one. Adjusted R-squared also accounts for how many predictors you use, so it only goes up when the new variables help enough.
